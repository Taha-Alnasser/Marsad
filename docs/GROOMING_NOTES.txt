high risk definition: The industry pattern for crisis alerts scores roughly three things. One framework describes it as severity is how bad it is, reach risk is how far it travels, with extra flags for the things that change the response rather than the grade. Others add velocity: a rapidly increasing rate of mentions suggesting viral spread potential, and amplification when high reach accounts pick up negative content. The repeated lesson is to alert on the spike, not the mention. One angry customer is not a crisis.

1. About us: mentions the entity, its leadership, a giga project, or visa and entry policy
2. Negative and serious: safety or harm to visitors, allegations (corruption, mistreatment), criticism from a foreign government or official body, misinformation about policy, project delay or cancellation
3. Amplified: published by a top tier or international outlet, OR the same story picked up by 3+ outlets

eval: Eval, specific to this case. Four layers:
1. Classification: hand label around 60 articles yourself, including Arabic ones and tricky cases. Binary pass/fail per field. For the alert, the costly mistake is missing a high risk story, so measure recall first and accept some false alarms. Say that tradeoff out loud in the presentation; it's a judgment call the panel will like.
2. Briefing faithfulness, claim by claim. Split the briefing into individual claims, each must cite an article. Then check each claim against its source. Research uses error types like these: a statement introducing facts not found in or verifiable by the article, an entity error such as a wrong name, number, or pronoun, and a statement that contradicts the article. Use that as your judge prompt. arxiv
3. A cheap deterministic check first. Tourism briefings are full of numbers (visitor counts, dates, project budgets). Every number in the briefing must appear in the cited article's text, checked in plain Python. No LLM needed, catches the worst hallucinations. A team doing financial news summarisation found the same thing: semantic proximity is not fact verification, and that when you are evaluating summaries across millions of assets, the first filter needs to be cheap. yldyld
4. Check the judge before trusting it. Run the LLM judge on your 60 labelled items and see how often it agrees with you. Research finds LLM judges achieve a strong correlation with human judgments on news faithfulness, but you show yours does on this data.
For the faithfulness judge, I adapted the error categories from FineSurE and UniSumEval: facts not in the article, wrong entity or number, contradiction. Then I checked the judge against my own labels before trusting it.
